Every laptop is already a sonar.

  • The speakers play near-inaudible sound at 18–21.5 kHz, the top edge of human hearing and the first range to fade with age.
  • The microphone hears its echoes off hands, bodies and a chest.
  • Motion shifts the echo's pitch (the Doppler effect). Breathing shifts its timing: at 20 kHz a wave is 1.7 cm long, so a 4 mm breath moves the echo by almost half a wave.
  • No new hardware. The speaker, microphone and processor are already in the room.

Sound out, echoes in, numbers out.

  1. 1EmitTwo pilot tones (18 and 18.5 kHz) and a 19–21.5 kHz chirp, 48 kHz audio
  2. 2ListenMic records the echoes, with Windows' voice filters switched off so ultrasound survives
  3. 3CleanRemove the speaker-to-mic leak and static room echoes
  4. 4ReadTone Doppler for hand motion; chirp echoes by distance for presence and breathing
  5. 5DecideA small CNN names the gesture and presence; filtering gives breaths per minute

Under the hood

Signal

  • Pilot tones at 18 and 18.5 kHz, one per speaker, plus a 19–21.5 kHz chirp every 20 ms
  • 48 kHz in WASAPI exclusive mode: in shared mode, Windows' mic processing erased every tone from 15 to 22 kHz
  • Measured on the demo laptop: about 55–60 dB tone SNR at 17–20 kHz

Processing

  • Speaker-to-mic leak and static echoes removed
  • 64-bin Doppler spectrogram per tone and mic (about ±2.3 m/s), 50 frames a second
  • Echoes in 5 cm steps out to 2.6 m; breathing from the echo phase at the chest, band-passed to 0.1–0.6 Hz

Model

  • Small CNN on Doppler spectrograms from 2 tones × 2 mics: four gestures plus "no gesture"
  • Presence from echo motion: empty, entering, present
  • ONNX on the laptop CPU, no GPU; calibration, thresholds and debouncing keep the live demo steady
