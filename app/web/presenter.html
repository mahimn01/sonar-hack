An ordinary laptop plays sound too high to hear and listens to the echoes. From them it measures a still person's breathing, notices when someone comes or goes, and reads hand gestures. No camera. Nothing to wear.

An ordinary laptop is already a sonar.

  • The speakers play near-inaudible sound at 18.5–21.5 kHz, the top edge of human hearing and the first range to fade with age.
  • The two microphones hear its echoes off hands, bodies and a chest.
  • Motion shifts the echo's pitch (the Doppler effect). Breathing shifts its timing: at 20 kHz a wave is 1.7 cm long, so a 4 mm breath moves the echo by almost half a wave.
  • No new hardware. The speaker, microphone and processor are already in the room.

Sound out, echoes in, numbers out.

  1. 1EmitA steady 18.5 kHz tone and a 19.5–21.5 kHz chirp, looping every 20 ms
  2. 2ListenBoth mics record at 48 kHz in exclusive mode. Windows' normal mode filters out everything above 15 kHz
  3. 3CleanCut speech and room noise, then the speaker-to-mic leak and still echoes
  4. 4ReadTone Doppler for hand motion. Chirp echoes by distance for presence and breathing
  5. 5DecideA small CNN names the gesture. Presence and breathing need no training

Under the hood

Signal

  • An 18.5 kHz tone on one speaker, a 19.5–21.5 kHz chirp on the other, every 20 ms
  • 48 kHz, WASAPI exclusive mode: shared mode erases 15–22 kHz
  • Self-test: tone 63 dB, chirp 73 dB above noise (1 Hz), drift −0.3 ppm, no audible leak

Processing

  • Speech filtered out first, then the speaker-to-mic leak and still echoes
  • 64-bin Doppler picture per mic (about ±2.3 m/s), 50 frames a second
  • Distance in 2.7 cm steps to 2.6 m. Breathing from echo phase: 1 mm of chest = 43°

Decisions

  • Gestures: a small CNN, pre-trained on simulated physics, fine-tuned on real recordings
  • Left and right from the two mics: both speaker channels reach them within 4 µs
  • Presence and breathing: signal processing, no training. Everything on the laptop CPU

From one laptop to every room

  • Clinical validationBreathing rate against a respiration belt and nurse counts, across ages, chairs and beds.
  • Overnight breathingAt the bedside, lying down and under a blanket, building on contactless sleep apnea research such as UW's ApneaApp (MobiSys 2015).
  • Fall detectionA fast movement whose distance changes by more than half a metre in under a second, then stillness.
  • Caregiver alertsAlerts and trends on a family member's or nurse's phone. Never footage.
  • Any speaker and micTablets, smart speakers and TVs already in the room, with the sonar inside the audio driver so it can share the speaker.
